Website Speed and Core Web Vitals: How They Affect SEO and Customers

22 August 20269 min readCodehouse Team
The short answer: A fast website does not bring customers by itself, but a slow or unstable page can drive them away before they read your offer. A proper assessment combines PageSpeed Insights, real-user data, mobile experience and conversion measurements.

If you have seen a low speed score and wonder whether it explains why your website does not bring enough calls or enquiries, the answer needs some nuance. The score is a useful diagnostic tool, not a grade that determines a site’s success on its own.

For a business in Athens, Thessaloniki, Crete or anywhere else in Greece, the goal is simple: the page should open quickly on a phone, not “jump” as it loads, respond when someone taps a button, and guide them easily to a call, booking, purchase or quote request. This guide explains how speed, Core Web Vitals and SEO are connected—and what is worth fixing first.

Why website speed matters to a business

Speed is part of the first impression. A visitor searching “restaurant in Athens,” “Santorini airport transfer” or “website development Thessaloniki” usually compares several options. If your site is slow, they may return to the results before seeing your services, photos, reviews or phone number.

The effect goes beyond bounce rate. Slow loading creates friction at every step: delaying a café menu, making a form difficult to complete, making a booking widget look broken and potentially creating doubts about the quality of the business itself.

So the right question is not just “what is my score?” It is: can the right visitor quickly understand my offer and take the next step?

What are Core Web Vitals?

Core Web Vitals are metrics that describe key aspects of user experience. They do not measure whether a site is beautiful or whether the copy answers the right search. They measure whether content appears promptly, whether the page responds and whether its layout remains stable.

LCP — Largest Contentful Paint

LCP shows how quickly the largest main element in the initial viewport appears, such as the heading, a hero image or a large block of text. As a general target, a good LCP is up to 2.5 seconds. An oversized uncompressed image, a slow server or CSS/JavaScript that blocks rendering are common causes of poor performance.

INP — Interaction to Next Paint

INP measures how quickly the page responds to user actions, such as tapping the mobile menu, clicking a CTA or submitting a form. A good INP is up to 200 milliseconds. Too many scripts, heavy third-party widgets and JavaScript blocking the main thread can make a site feel “stuck.”

CLS — Cumulative Layout Shift

CLS measures whether elements move unexpectedly while the page loads. If a visitor tries to tap “Book” and the button moves because an image without dimensions loads, the experience is poor. A good CLS is up to 0.1. Reserving space for images, fonts and embeds reduces these shifts.

PageSpeed Insights: what the score really means

PageSpeed Insights combines lab tests with, when enough data is available, real-user data from the Chrome User Experience Report. These two kinds of data answer different questions:

  • Lab data: a controlled simulation that helps identify technical problems during the test.
  • Field data: the experience of real visitors on different devices, connections and in different locations.

A score of 95 does not guarantee more customers, just as a score of 65 does not mean the site is useless. But if real users struggle on mobile, the form does not respond or the main content is slow, there is a business problem worth fixing regardless of the number.

Measure the homepage, key service pages and contact page separately. A fast page does not “save” a heavy booking page or slow e-shop checkout.

7 practical ways to speed up your website

1. Optimise images before uploading them

Large photos are among the most common causes of a slow website. Use WebP or AVIF where supported, resize to the dimensions actually displayed and compress without sacrificing necessary quality. A hero image does not need to be several megabytes.

2. Load below-the-fold content only when needed

Gallery images, maps, videos and embeds lower down the page can use lazy loading. The main content in the first viewport should have priority; non-essential elements should not compete with the heading and primary CTA.

3. Reduce scripts and third-party widgets

Analytics, chat boxes, social feeds, booking systems and trackers add value only when needed. Check what scripts load on each page and remove those that are unused. Where possible, load them after the main content or only on the page that needs them.

4. Improve server response, caching and hosting

If the server is slow to send the first byte, browser optimisation is not enough. Check caching, compression, PHP/runtime configuration, database queries where applicable and hosting quality. For visitors on islands or in different parts of Greece, consistent response on real connections matters more than a score from an ideal lab.

5. Keep CSS and fonts lightweight

Many stylesheets, unused frameworks and large font files delay the first render. Use only the font weights you need, declare suitable fallback fonts and remove unused CSS. Good visual design does not need to be sacrificed for speed; it needs better prioritisation.

6. Set stable dimensions for media

Set width and height on images, reserve space for ads or embeds and avoid layout changes as fonts or components load. This reduces CLS and helps users keep their place while reading or tapping.

7. Treat mobile as a separate product

Mobile is not simply a smaller desktop version. Test on a real smartphone with a moderate connection: open the menu, tap the phone number, complete the form and scroll. If the key action is not comfortable to complete, speed-score improvements need to be combined with UX improvements.

Speed, SEO and GEO searches

Speed helps SEO because it makes a page more useful and accessible, but it does not replace content. To appear in searches such as “website for café Athens,” “web design Thessaloniki” or “hotel online bookings Crete,” a page needs to clearly explain the service, location and next step.

The same applies to GEO (Generative Engine Optimisation), or optimising content so it is easy to understand and cite in AI answers. There is no magic button that gets you into every AI answer. Good practices include:

  • Direct answers: begin important sections with a clear answer, then explain.
  • Specific entities: state what you offer, where and for what kind of business.
  • Logical structure: use descriptive H2/H3 headings, short paragraphs, lists and FAQs where genuinely useful.
  • Consistent details: keep business name, phone, area, services and links consistent across your site and business profiles.
  • Evidence and accuracy: prefer real projects, current information and honest wording over generic promises.

A fast site makes all this information easier to access. Structure, usefulness and credibility give visitors a reason to see and trust it. Speed is only one part of optimisation; for the other technical and on-page factors, see our website SEO guide.

A checklist for Greek businesses

Before simply asking to “raise the speed score,” check these items on mobile and desktop:

  1. Do the heading and core value proposition load without a long wait?
  2. Do the phone number, CTA, menu and form display correctly?
  3. Are there heavy images or videos that are not needed in the first viewport?
  4. Do buttons, text or forms move as the page loads?
  5. Do third-party scripts load on pages where they add no value?
  6. Do service pages answer the customer’s search and location?
  7. Do you measure calls, forms, bookings or purchases before and after changes?

The right priority is usually: first, anything that blocks contact; then major performance improvements; finally, small score points that do not change the experience. For more conversion issues, also read 7 reasons your website is not bringing in customers.

Speed and SEO FAQs

What are Core Web Vitals?

They are the LCP, INP and CLS metrics describing how quickly the main content appears, how responsive the page is to user actions, and how stable the layout is.

What is a good PageSpeed score?

The score is an indicator, not the only goal. What matters is that real users—especially on mobile—get a good experience and can easily complete the action the business wants.

Does speed affect SEO?

Page experience and Core Web Vitals are among the signals Google considers, but they need to accompany useful content, sound SEO structure, credibility and relevance to the search.

How can I improve site speed quickly?

Start with image compression, WebP or AVIF, lazy loading, fewer scripts, proper caching and a hosting check. Measure the mobile homepage, service pages and forms separately.

Is your site actually slow?

Do not chase a number alone. Check the real journey from search to call, form, booking or purchase.
