7 Reasons Your Website Is Not Bringing You Customers

21 August 20268 min readCodehouse Team
The point: Visits alone are not enough. To bring customers, a website needs to load quickly, work comfortably on mobile, answer the intent behind a search and show visitors clearly what to do next.

A website may look polished and still fail to lead to calls, quote requests or bookings. That does not necessarily mean “the internet does not work” or that you need a complete rebuild. Often there are specific obstacles in the visitor’s journey: slow loading, confusing content, poor targeting or a lack of information that builds trust.

Below are seven common reasons, with the problem, its effect on the business and a practical first step to fix it. These principles apply to a local business in Athens or Thessaloniki, a shop on an island, a freelancer or an e-shop serving all of Greece.

1. The website is slow

The problem

When a page takes too long to display its main content, visitors wait in front of a blank or half-loaded screen. Common causes include very large photos, too many scripts and plugins, old hosting or code that loads things not needed on every page.

Why it affects the business

Someone searching for “plumber near me,” a table for tonight or an immediate solution on a phone has no reason to wait. They leave and try the next result. Delays reduce the chance of contact and also affect the experience search engines assess. It is not only about Google; it is about the first impression.

What you can do

Check speed with tools such as PageSpeed Insights and start with the obvious: compress photos to WebP or AVIF, use appropriate image dimensions, remove unnecessary plugins and external widgets, and make sure hosting has proper caching. Measure the homepage, key service pages and mobile version separately—not just one general score.

2. It does not work properly on mobile

The problem

Mobile-friendly does not simply mean the page “fits” a small screen. Text that requires zooming, buttons too close together, hard-to-fill forms, menus that do not open or phone numbers that cannot be tapped are signs that the experience was not designed for mobile.

Why it affects the business

For many local searches, the first visit comes from a smartphone. Someone may find you while walking through town, travelling in Crete or comparing professionals from the sofa. If they cannot quickly understand what you offer or call you, interest is lost before they see your work.

What you can do

Test the site on real phones, not only by shrinking the browser on a computer. Prioritize a readable font, enough space around buttons, a click-to-call phone number and a short form. Also check whether the main message, opening hours or service area are visible without endless scrolling.

3. It offers a poor user experience

The problem

Poor user experience occurs when visitors have to think too hard about basics: what the business actually does, which services it offers, whether it serves their area and how to get in touch. Long introductory copy, generic headings such as “Welcome,” complicated menus and distracting visual elements get in the way.

Why it affects the business

Confusion turns into delay. If visitors cannot find the information they need in a few seconds, they are unlikely to look deeper. This is especially true of services with many options—from law and technical offices to clinics, hotels and transport companies.

What you can do

Give every key page a clear purpose. The homepage should immediately explain what you offer, who you serve and where you operate. Service pages should answer specific questions and the menu should include only what is needed. Ask someone unfamiliar with the business to find a service and contact you; if they struggle, that is useful feedback.

4. It lacks a sound SEO structure

The problem

A page without a proper title, unique meta description, one clear H1 and a logical H2/H3 hierarchy makes it harder for search engines to understand its subject. The same is true of duplicate content, broken links, pages without canonical URLs or inconsistent business details.

Why it affects the business

SEO is not a technical label you add at the end. It is how you organize content so a search engine—and ultimately a person—understands what the page offers. If Google cannot connect the service, location and user intent with your content, you lose organic visibility for searches that could have commercial value.

What you can do

Run a basic technical check: one clear title per page, descriptive URL, correct H1, subheadings that organize real content, working internal links and a sitemap. For businesses with a physical presence, keep the business name, address and phone consistent on the website and Google Business Profile. The business SEO guide explains the basics in more detail.

5. It targets the wrong searches

The problem

Many websites speak mainly in the business’s own terms rather than the customer’s language. They may describe a service technically, while the interested person is searching for a solution: “accountant for starting a business Athens,” “air conditioner repair Piraeus” or “transfer from Santorini airport.” Others target very broad terms without answering a specific need.

Why it affects the business

Visits from people with no intent to become customers rarely convert. A good page does not simply chase more people; it tries to be useful to the right person at the moment they are searching. For a Greek local business, the service and location are often as important as the brand.

What you can do

Record the questions you hear in calls and messages. Review search terms in Google Search Console and use natural language to describe services, use cases and service areas. Create separate pages only when they offer real value—not dozens of near-identical pages that simply change a city name.

6. It lacks clear calls to action

The problem

A call to action (CTA) is the next step you ask a visitor to take: “Call us,” “Request a quote,” “Book an appointment,” “View the menu” or “Check availability.” Without this guidance, a visitor may be interested in the business but never take the next step.

Why it affects the business

The site becomes an informational brochure instead of a communication tool. Especially when visitors come from a phone, the distance between interest and action should be short. A hidden phone number, an empty contact page or a generic “Learn more” button is not enough.

What you can do

Choose one primary action for each page and label the button with a specific outcome. For example, a plumber’s page might say “Call for immediate service,” while a hotel’s might say “Check availability.” Put the action near the information that supports it, repeat it discreetly where needed and make sure it works on mobile.

7. It does not build enough trust with visitors

The problem

A site may list services and include a contact form, but fail to show who is behind the business or why someone should choose it. If it lacks real photos, work samples, clear contact details, reviews, terms or information about the working process, visitors have reasons to hesitate.

Why it affects the business

The decision is not purely rational. A customer comparing two similar options will often prefer the one that seems clearer, more genuine and more responsible. This matters in high-trust professions, online purchases and tourism, where visitors cannot see the service in person before paying.

What you can do

Use real evidence: portfolio, case studies, photos of the space or team, authentic reviews where permitted and a clear explanation of the process. Make contact details, privacy policy and necessary terms easy to find. Do not invent customers, numbers or reviews; credibility takes time to build but is quickly lost when something seems exaggerated or untrue.

Where to start

You do not need to fix all seven at once. Start with what prevents a customer from taking a basic action: speed, mobile function and visible contact details. Then check whether each page targets a real need and includes the information someone needs to trust the business. If you need broader technical and on-page improvements, explore website SEO; if the site itself needs a redesign, consider business website design.

Keep measurements from before and after changes—enquiries, calls, bookings or completed purchases, depending on your goal. This lets you assess what really helped rather than relying only on whether the site “looks better.”
